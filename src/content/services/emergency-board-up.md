## What Emergency Board Up actually involves

Board up is the stopgap between the event and the repair: a fire department breaches a door, a storm shatters a picture window, a break-in leaves a frame splintered, or a vehicle takes out a garage door, and the opening has to be closed off the same day. The work uses exterior-grade plywood or OSB cut to the exact opening, anchored into the structural frame rather than just the trim, plus tarping for roof penetrations or missing siding. On commercial storefronts this often means steel security panels instead of wood. The goal is two things at once: keep weather and unauthorized entry out, and keep the opening documented and intact for the adjuster who will see it next.

## Our process

1. **Hazard and access check.** Before anyone touches a board, the crew checks for live electrical, unstable framing, broken glass, or fire department lock-out tags that change how the opening can be approached.
2. **Measure and cut for the actual opening.** Boards are cut to the frame, not the hole, so the panel seats against structural members instead of flexing loose siding or drywall.
3. **Anchor into framing, not just trim.** Lag bolts or structural screws go into studs, headers, or masonry anchors so wind load and attempted re-entry don't pop the panel off overnight.
4. **Weatherproof the seams.** Tarping, flashing tape, or sealant closes gaps at the edges so wind-driven rain doesn't track behind the board and start a second water loss inside a wall that's already compromised.
5. **Photograph before and after.** Every opening gets documented prior to securing, which becomes part of the claim file and protects against disputes over pre-existing damage later.

## What separates a good board up response from a bad one

The most common failure is using finish nails or a few screws into trim boards, which holds for a day and then peels back in the first windstorm. Another is skipping the interior side entirely, leaving a board that looks solid from the street but has a gap at the sill where water pools and starts soaking subfloor or framing nobody is watching yet. On fire losses, crews that board up without photographing the opening first often create a dispute later about whether damage to the frame happened during the fire or during the board up itself. Adjusters also look for whether the board up matches the opening size documented in the initial loss report. A mismatch, even an honest one, slows down approval of the next phase of work.

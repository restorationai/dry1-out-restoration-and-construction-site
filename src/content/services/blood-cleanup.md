Blood cleanup is rarely something anyone plans for. Whether it follows a medical emergency, an accident, an unattended death, or a violent incident, the aftermath involves more than visible cleanup: bloodborne pathogens can remain active on and inside porous materials long after a scene looks clean to the eye. This page explains what the process actually involves, so you know what to expect before a crew arrives.

## What Blood Cleanup actually involves

Blood and other bodily fluids are treated as a biohazard risk because they can carry pathogens that aren't visible or detectable by smell. The work goes well beyond wiping a surface. Technicians assess which materials are porous (carpet padding, subfloor, drywall, upholstery) and which are not, because porous materials that have absorbed fluid generally cannot be fully decontaminated and need to be removed rather than cleaned. Non-porous surfaces, tile, sealed hardwood, countertops, metal fixtures, are cleaned and disinfected in place using hospital-grade products. Timelines vary with the size of the affected area and how many rooms or materials are involved, but most residential jobs are scoped and underway within hours of the initial call, with disposal and final disinfection often completing the same day or the next.

## Our process

1. **PPE staging and scene containment.** Before any work starts, technicians suit up in protective equipment and section off the affected area to prevent cross-contamination to the rest of the home.
2. **Material assessment.** Porous materials that have absorbed fluid are identified for removal; non-porous surfaces are flagged for in-place cleaning and disinfection.
3. **Removal and extraction.** Contaminated flooring, padding, and other non-salvageable materials are removed and bagged according to biohazard handling practices.
4. **Cleaning and disinfection.** Remaining surfaces are treated with hospital-grade disinfectants, followed by a second pass to confirm nothing was missed.
5. **Odor control and final inspection.** Air scrubbing and odor neutralization address any residual smell, and a final walkthrough confirms the space is safe to re-enter.

## What separates a good blood cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake in blood cleanup isn't visible cleaning, it's under-removal. A surface can look spotless while the subfloor or carpet padding beneath it still holds absorbed fluid, which creates both a lingering health risk and an odor problem that shows up days later. Operators who rush the job often skip the step of checking beneath flooring or behind baseboards where fluid can travel. Insurance adjusters reviewing these claims typically want clear documentation: photos before and after, a list of materials removed, and disposal records. A thorough response documents every step for the claim file and treats disposal procedures as part of the job, not an afterthought handled off the books.

## What's the right order of calls for a sewage backup?

Stop using water fixtures first, then call a restoration company for the contamination and a plumber for the clog, and only call the city if the problem looks like it's coming from the public main. Each call solves a different part of the problem, and doing them out of order either wastes time or lets contaminated water spread further.

A plumber's job is the pipe: finding and clearing whatever is blocking the line, whether that's roots, grease, or a collapsed section. A restoration company's job is everything the sewage touched: the standing water, the saturated flooring, the walls, and the air quality. Many Hayward households call both at once rather than waiting for the plumber to finish before starting cleanup, since Category 3 water keeps doing damage the longer it sits.

Your insurer comes last, after the water is out and the damage is documented. Filing a claim before mitigation starts just delays the adjuster's visit while your home sits wet.

## When is it a plumber's job versus a restoration company's job?

Call a plumber first when the backup is limited to one fixture and hasn't spread, such as a toilet that keeps bubbling but the water stays inside the bowl. That's a line-clearing job, and a plumber can often resolve it the same day.

Call a restoration company immediately once sewage has reached the floor, baseboards, carpet, or any finished surface. At that point you're dealing with Category 3 water under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), which means grossly contaminated water that can carry bacteria and viruses. It needs PPE, containment, and usually removal of porous materials like carpet, pad, and sometimes drywall rather than drying in place. Dry 1 Out handles the extraction, antimicrobial treatment, and structural drying side of this; the plumber still needs to find and fix whatever caused the backup so it doesn't happen again next week.

## When is it the City of Hayward's problem, not mine?

It's worth reporting to the city when the backup seems tied to the public sewer main rather than your home's private lateral line, which is usually the case when neighboring homes on the same block are backing up around the same time. A single-home backup from a clogged kitchen line or a tree-root-choked lateral is almost always a private plumbing issue, which falls on the homeowner (or landlord) to fix, not the city.

Either way, reporting a suspected main-line issue doesn't replace cleanup. The water in your home is still Category 3 contamination regardless of where it came from, and it still needs to be extracted and treated the same day.

## What should I do in the first hour, before anyone arrives?

Stop using every water fixture in the house, including the dishwasher, washing machine, and every toilet, the moment you notice a backup. Adding more water pushes the existing sewage further and makes the eventual cleanup bigger.

Keep people and pets out of the affected area. Category 3 water isn't something to wade through or mop up yourself; the [EPA's guidance on flooded homes](https://www.epa.gov/flooded-homes) is written for exactly this kind of contaminated-water situation, and the short version is the same: limit contact, ventilate if it's safe to open a window, and don't track it through the rest of the house.

Photograph the damage before anyone starts moving things, if you can do it without stepping into standing water. Timestamped photos of the affected rooms, the source fixture, and any damaged belongings are what your insurer will ask for first. ## What happens when the restoration crew arrives?

A technician in full PPE sets up containment first, then extracts standing water and begins moisture readings on the walls and subfloor to map how far the contamination traveled. That mapping decides what gets treated in place versus what has to come out.

Have your insurance information and the location of your main water shut-off ready when the crew arrives; it speeds up both the documentation and the cleanup. Expect carpet, pad, and sometimes baseboard or drywall sections to be removed rather than dried, since porous materials that touched Category 3 water generally can't be fully decontaminated. The crew applies antimicrobial treatment, sets up air movers and dehumidifiers for structural drying, and documents everything for your claim. Check your policy's water backup or sewer backup endorsement before assuming it's covered; standard homeowners policies typically exclude sewer and sump backups unless that endorsement was added, a point confirmed in the [Insurance Information Institute's guide to what standard homeowners insurance covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance).

Once the plumber confirms the line is clear and the crew has extracted and treated the affected areas, the drying phase typically runs several days before reconstruction, if needed, can start.

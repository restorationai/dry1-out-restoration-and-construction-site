## When do you call a restoration company instead of a plumber?

Call a plumber first only if water is actively running, a pipe is actively leaking, or you can still hear water moving behind a wall. Shut off the source at the valve if you can reach it safely, then make the second call to a restoration company.

Mold can start growing on wet material within 24 to 48 hours, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), so once the water stops, time matters more than the mold's size. A restoration crew handles both the leftover moisture and the mold growth it caused, which is why [water damage restoration](/services/water-damage-restoration/) and [mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/) are often booked as one visit rather than two separate companies.

If the water came from a sewer backup or a Category 3 (grossly contaminated) source, skip the plumber-only route entirely and call a restoration company first. That water carries contamination the mold crew needs to account for in the cleanup plan.

## Do you need a mold inspector before remediation, or can you go straight to a remediation crew?

Small, visible patches of mold on a hard, non-porous surface can often be cleaned by the property owner without a separate inspection, while larger areas usually call for a professional assessment first. The EPA draws the line at roughly 10 square feet, about a 3-foot by 3-foot patch, as the point where a DIY wipe-down is no longer the right approach (see the [EPA's mold cleanup guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home)).

Above that size, or if the mold followed a flood, a slow leak you can't locate, or a smell with no visible source, a [mold inspection and testing](/services/mold-inspection-testing/) visit comes first. An inspector uses a moisture meter and sometimes air sampling to map how far the problem has traveled behind walls or under flooring before anyone starts demolition. That scope becomes the basis for the remediation plan and for any insurance claim.

## If you rent in Vista, when do you put it in writing to your landlord?

As soon as you find mold, not after you've waited to see if it gets worse. California's habitability standards put the burden on landlords to address moisture and mold conditions that affect a unit's livability, and a written notice, even a simple email with photos and the date, is what protects a tenant if the landlord is slow to respond.

Keep a copy of that notice and any photos you send. If the landlord doesn't arrange an inspection or remediation within a reasonable window, that paper trail matters whether you end up contacting code enforcement, a tenant rights organization, or legal counsel. A restoration company can document the extent of the mold for that file, but the written notice to the landlord has to come from you.

## Who pays for mold remediation: insurance, you, or the landlord?

For homeowners, a standard policy typically covers mold removal only when it resulted from a sudden, covered water event, like a burst supply line, not from a long-term leak or ongoing humidity. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) outlines how gradual damage and maintenance issues are usually excluded, and even covered mold claims often carry a lower coverage cap than the rest of the policy.

For renters, the landlord is generally responsible for remediating mold tied to a building defect (a roof leak, a plumbing failure, poor ventilation built into the unit), while a renters policy mainly protects the tenant's own belongings, not the structure itself. For California health guidance on indoor mold exposure, the [California Department of Public Health](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CCDPHP/DEODC/EHLB/IAQ/Pages/Mold.aspx) is a useful reference if you're also weighing health concerns alongside the repair question.

Every policy and every lease reads differently, so this is general guidance, not a promise of what your specific claim will pay.

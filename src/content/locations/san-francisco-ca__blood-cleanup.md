## Why San Francisco's Housing Stock Shapes Blood Cleanup Work

Much of San Francisco's residential stock predates modern building codes: narrow Victorian and Edwardian staircases, original hardwood subfloors, and tongue-and-groove flooring that was never designed to be sealed against liquid intrusion. In neighborhoods like Noe Valley and Bernal Heights, those older floors and baseboards can allow bodily fluids to migrate into subfloor gaps and wall cavities faster than a modern sealed floor would. In denser multi-unit buildings across SoMa and the Mission District (94110), shared hallways and tight stairwells mean we plan equipment staging and material removal routes before we ever bring in containment barriers, so neighbors and building traffic are affected as little as possible.

## Our Blood Cleanup Process in San Francisco

Every job starts with an assessment of affected materials: flooring, subfloor, drywall, and any porous furnishings that cannot be fully decontaminated. We establish containment, remove and bag biohazard waste per regulated medical waste handling protocols, then apply EPA-registered disinfectants and verify the area meets our internal clearance standards before removing containment. In older San Francisco buildings where plaster or original wood flooring is present, we're more conservative about what gets cut out versus treated in place, since replacement materials matching a pre-1940s building can be harder to source and more disruptive to install.

## Privacy and Building Coordination

A large share of San Francisco's housing is multi-unit, which means HOA boards, property managers, or building superintendents are often part of the conversation even when the unit itself is privately owned. We coordinate elevator reservations, freight access, and service-entrance timing directly with building management so a crew in uniform and marked vehicles isn't moving through a lobby during peak foot traffic. For walk-up buildings without elevator access, common in parts of the Richmond District and Sunset District, we plan equipment carry routes in advance rather than improvising on arrival.

## Local note

In pre-1950 San Francisco buildings, bathroom and kitchen subfloors were frequently built with tongue-and-groove fir over joists rather than plywood underlayment. That seam pattern lets fluids wick sideways along the grain instead of pooling, which means the visible affected area on the surface is often smaller than what's actually impacted underneath. We check subfloor moisture and staining a few feet beyond the obvious boundary before calling a floor section clear.
